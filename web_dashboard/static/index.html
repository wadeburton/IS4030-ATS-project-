Do the two models agree? — rank agreement surface

Every resume is placed by where it ranks under TF-IDF (one floor axis) and under MiniLM (the other), from worst to best. Ranks are used instead of raw scores because the two models score on different scales — TF-IDF scores sit near 0 for almost everyone, so raw scores can't be compared directly. The floor is a 12×12 grid; height and color show how many resumes fall in each cell.

How to read it: a ridge running corner to corner (worst–worst to best–best) means the models rank resumes the same way. Bumps away from that diagonal are resumes one model likes and the other doesn't — the cases worth a human look. Flat, evenly spread terrain means little agreement. Drag to rotate.

Test 2 · Keyword stuffing

Each out-of-field control resume (nursing, sales) gets the posting's 12 most distinctive words pasted onto the end as a fake "skills" line — nothing else changes. A screener that is hard to game should keep these resumes out of the top 20.

Control resumes in the top 20, before and after stuffing

How the tests are run: both models are frozen — TF-IDF is fitted once on the unchanged resumes and never refitted — and each changed resume is ranked against the other, unchanged resumes. Resumes are shortened slightly first so the added text is never cut off. Names are synthetic. These results describe this sample and these two methods; they are not evidence of demographic discrimination or of real hiring outcomes.

      How it works

      Both scoring methods answer the same question — how similar is this resume's text to this job posting's text? — but they read the text in opposite ways.

      TF-IDF: counts shared words

      TF-IDF (term frequency–inverse document frequency) treats each text as a bag of words. A word that appears in both the resume and the posting adds to the score, and a word that is rare across the 300 resumes (like "tableau") counts for much more than a common one (like "team").

      It has no idea what any word means. "Built reports in Power BI" and "created dashboards in Tableau" share almost no words, so they barely match.

      MiniLM: compares meaning

      MiniLM (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) is a small neural network that was already trained on more than a billion sentence pairs. It reads the words in context and turns each text into 384 numbers that summarise what the text is about. Texts about similar things get similar numbers.

      It can match related experience written in different words, but nobody can point to the exact words that produced a score.

      Limits to keep in mind

      • Scores measure how similar two texts are. They are not hiring probabilities and not accuracy.
      • No human rated any resume, so the study cannot name a "correct" ranking.
      • The 300 resumes and 20 postings are a sample of public Kaggle data in one job family (data analyst and BI roles).
      • Names in the name test are made up. The test shows instability, not bias against a group.
